# Natural-language decipherment claims for the Voynich manuscript, and how they were received

Notes compiled 2026-10-17 15:01 PDT (clock read from shell). Sources were web pages fetched or searched in this session. Where a fact comes only from a search-engine summary, not a page I fetched, it is marked "(search summary)". Where I could not confirm something, it says "unverified". voynich.nu's solutions page returned 404 at the URL I tried, and Ars Technica, the Annual Review of Linguistics and the Bowern/Lindemann PDF could not be fetched. Zandbergen's and Davis's views below are therefore taken from secondary quotations.

## Which language claims have attracted the most attention, and how was each received?

### Takeaway
No natural-language claim from Newbold (1921) to October 2026 has been accepted by Voynich specialists. The claims that got the most press were Bax (2014), Tucker & Talbert (2013/14), Hauer & Kondrak (TACL 2016, publicised 2018), Gibbs (TLS 2017), Ardıç (2018 onward) and Cheshire (Romance Studies 2019). All were rejected for the same few reasons: the mapping has too many degrees of freedom, the output is not grammatical, the results cannot be reproduced, or the claim ignores the text's statistics. Only Hauer & Kondrak and Cheshire passed formal peer review. In both cases critics said the reviewers were not Voynich or Hebrew specialists, or that the journal was the wrong venue.

### Cited Findings

**Catalogue: peer-reviewed or formal-venue claims**

- **Hauer & Kondrak (University of Alberta). Paper TACL vol. 4, 2016; press push January 2018. Claimed language: Hebrew, written as alphagrams (letters of each word sorted alphabetically) under a monoalphabetic substitution.**
  - Method: They built language-identification methods for substitution ciphers. The best one was 97% accurate across 380 languages, and on the Voynich text it "suggest[s] Hebrew" — [ACL Anthology Q16-1006](https://aclanthology.org/Q16-1006/).
  - The test set was the Universal Declaration of Human Rights in 380 languages. Under one positional method, Hebrew beat the other 379 — [search summary citing Mosaic](https://mosaicmagazine.com/observation/2018/02/no-the-mysterious-voynich-manuscript-is-not-written-in-hebrew/).
  - The team first suspected Arabic, but the algorithm pointed to Hebrew. When the first line was unscrambled as alphagrams, about 80% of the resulting words were in a Hebrew dictionary — [search summary; ScienceAlert / Smithsonian coverage](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/artificial-intelligence-takes-crack-mysterious-voynich-manuscript-180967983/).
  - Sample reading: "She made recommendations to the priest, man of the house and me and people." It was produced after "a couple of spelling corrections" and a pass through Google Translate — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript); [search summary](https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/voynich-manuscript-artificial-intelligence-solved).
  - Coverage: the published readings cover roughly the first line or sentence only. The authors did not claim a full decipherment. Kondrak said identifying Hebrew was only "the first step" — [search summary](https://www.ualberta.ca/science-news/2018/january/ai-used-to-decipher-ancient-manuscript).
  - Conflict on dates: Wikipedia dates the claim to "August 2017" at the ACL annual meeting and also cites TACL 2016 — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript). The ACL Anthology lists the TACL paper as Q16-1006 (2016).
  - **René Zandbergen's rejection:** "The Voynich MS has a number of features that are not addressed in the paper. The most important one is that the character bigram entropy is anomalously low." Anagramming would be the only way this scenario could produce that, and "earlier experiments in this direction have not been successful." He also found the anagrammed-substitution assumption unconvincing and noted that the results depend on which transcription is used — [Klausis Krypto Kolumne, 2018-02-05](https://scienceblogs.de/klausis-krypto-kolumne/2018/02/05/voynich-manuscript-errrm-no-not-really-sorry-but/).
  - **Nick Pelling's rejection:** many people had proposed similar ideas before, and the paper lacked a related-work discussion — [same](https://scienceblogs.de/klausis-krypto-kolumne/2018/02/05/voynich-manuscript-errrm-no-not-really-sorry-but/). A search summary also attributes to Pelling the view that the paper's chance of being correct is "essentially zero" (unverified; I did not open the source page).
  - **Jürgen Hermes's rejection:** the authors ignore important properties of the text — [same](https://scienceblogs.de/klausis-krypto-kolumne/2018/02/05/voynich-manuscript-errrm-no-not-really-sorry-but/).
  - **Other critics:** Medievalist Damian Fleming objected to using Google Translate instead of a Hebrew scholar. Elonka Dunin said "Everyone sees what they want to see." Shlomo Argamon said the method allows "huge latitude" for impressionistic interpretation. The Mosaic critic said that even after the spelling corrections, the Hebrew words do not mean what was claimed — [search summaries of Atlas Obscura / Mosaic](https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/voynich-manuscript-artificial-intelligence-solved).

- **Gerard Cheshire (honorary research associate, University of Bristol). May 2019. Claimed language: "calligraphic proto-Romance".**
  - Venue: *Romance Studies*, "The Language and Writing System of MS408 (Voynich) Explained", doi:10.1080/02639904.2019.1599566 — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript). It passed double-blind peer review — [ZME Science](https://zmescience.com/science/british-scolar-voynich-manuscript-decoded-0423432). A follow-up book, *The Medieval Map and the Mercy Mission*, came out in 2023 — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript).
  - Method: a mixed alphabet with no punctuation, all lowercase, no double consonants, and "diphthongs, triphthongs, quadriphthongs and quintiphthongs", plus some Latin words and abbreviations. He said the decipherment took about two weeks, through "lateral thinking and ingenuity". His story is that Dominican nuns wrote it as a reference work for Maria of Castile, Queen of Aragon — [ZME Science](https://zmescience.com/science/british-scolar-voynich-manuscript-decoded-0423432); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript).
  - Sample readings, from a bathing-women caption: *tozosr* "buzzing: too noisy", *orla la* "on the edge: losing patience", *tolora* "silly/foolish", *noror* "cloudy: dull/sad", *or aus* "golden bird: well behaved", *oleios* "oiled: slippery". *Palina* was read as a stadia rod. The foldout was read as a map showing a volcano near the island of Vulcano — [ZME Science](https://zmescience.com/science/british-scolar-voynich-manuscript-decoded-0423432); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript).
  - Coverage: a handful of labels and phrases. A full translation and lexicon were "planned" — [ZME Science](https://zmescience.com/science/british-scolar-voynich-manuscript-decoded-0423432).
  - **Lisa Fagin Davis's rejection** (to Ars Technica): "the logic of this proposal is circular and aspirational". Also: "Sorry, folks, 'proto-Romance language' is not a thing." She called the premise of a single proto-Romance language "completely unsubstantiated" and at odds with paleolinguistics — [ZME Science](https://zmescience.com/science/british-scolar-voynich-manuscript-decoded-0423432); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript).
  - **J. K. Petersen's objections:** many nonsensical word combinations; almost no grammar; a letter distribution unlike Romance languages; drawings that would fit other readings just as well; the same word read differently in different places; and no explanation of why a mid-15th-century scribe would use an undocumented script — [ZME Science](https://zmescience.com/science/british-scolar-voynich-manuscript-decoded-0423432).
  - A Göttingen university statement said there is no scientific evidence for a unified proto-Romance language — [search summary](https://www.uni-goettingen.de/de/608250.html).
  - **Institutional fallout:** Bristol removed its press release and said the work was "not affiliated with the University of Bristol" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript); [Higgypop](https://higgypop.com/news/bristol-uni-voynich-manuscript). Davis argued the reviewers were probably Romance specialists, not Voynich specialists — [Undark, 2020-02-12](https://undark.org/2020/02/12/decoding-bizarre-voynich-manuscript/).
  - One search summary mislabelled the claim as "2024". The paper is from 2019.

- **Arthur Tucker & Rexford Talbert (later with Jules Janick). HerbalGram 2013/2014; book 2018. Claimed language: an extinct dialect of Nahuatl (central Mexico, possibly Morelos or Puebla), with the plants identified as New World species.**
  - Method: they identified 37 plants, 6 animals and 1 mineral by comparison with the Badianus manuscript (1552). They dated the manuscript to 1521–c.1576, which conflicts with the radiocarbon date — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript); [search summary](https://ciphermysteries.com/?p=11719).
  - The authors themselves said "much, much more work needs to be done" — (search summary).
  - **Magnus Pharao Hansen (Nahuatl specialist) rejected it** as "pure speculation" and "pseudo-rigorous" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript); [search summary of Cipher Mysteries](https://ciphermysteries.com/?p=11719).
  - Hansen noted that more than half the proposed plant names begin with the letter read as /a/. When he applied the 2018 key, the output was repetitive, more like glossolalia than Nahuatl — (search summary of Cipher Mysteries, unverified at the page level).
  - Data-focused scientists called the approach circular — [Undark](https://undark.org/2020/02/12/decoding-bizarre-voynich-manuscript/).

- **Ahmet Ardıç and family ("Ata Team Alberta"). Announced February 2018. Claimed language: Old Turkic, written in a "poetic" style with phonemic orthography.**
  - Method: they matched glyph combinations to Old Turkic words, mainly using Clauson's etymological dictionary. The team claims 60+ "combination" glyphs — [Cipher Mysteries, 2018-02-28](https://ciphermysteries.com/?p=10783).
  - Coverage claimed: "over 30%" of the manuscript and "at least 300 words" — [Cipher Mysteries](https://ciphermysteries.com/?p=10783); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript).
  - Venue: YouTube video, then the Conference on Turkish World Studies. A submission to *Digital Philology* was rejected in 2019. A later 195-page paper is on turkicresearch.com — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript).
  - Reception: Davis, "mildly pressed" by Toronto Metro in 2018, called it "one of the few solutions I've seen that is consistent, is repeatable, and results in sensical text". Pelling mocked that remark and called the team's correspondence tables "tricksy" — [Cipher Mysteries](https://ciphermysteries.com/?p=10783).
  - Koen Gheuens likened it to goropism and the Sun Language Theory — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript). The Turkish side says he released a debunking video in 2025 — (search summary).
  - J. K. Petersen asked for the 60+ combination glyphs, which had not been shared — [Cipher Mysteries](https://ciphermysteries.com/?p=10783).
  - **2026 update:** a February 2026 Voynich Ninja thread (posts dated 24-02-2026) presented a third article from a board affiliated with the Turkish Cultural Research Institute, chaired by Ahmet Bican Ercilasun, which concluded the text is Turkish. The thread says this board's first peer-reviewed paper was in 2023. Forum members confirmed it is the same theory as Ardıç's. One member called it "largely speculative based on loose similarities", with no convincing translated passage — [Voynich Ninja tid=5401](https://www.voynich.ninja/printthread.php?tid=5401). I could not see the journal or authors (behind a login), so the venue is unverified.

- **Nicholas Gibbs. *Times Literary Supplement*, September 2017. Claimed language: idiosyncratically abbreviated Latin, giving a largely plagiarised guide to women's health.**
  - Method: he read Voynich glyphs as Latin abbreviation and ligature marks, for example the "et" sign — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript); [search summary](https://boingboing.net/2017/09/11/nice-try.html).
  - The technical evidence was two lines shown in a small, barely legible image — [Cipher Mysteries](https://ciphermysteries.com/?p=10022).
  - Venue: the TLS is a literary weekly. This was not peer review.
  - **Davis's rejection:** the decipherment "doesn't result in Latin that makes sense", and she was "surprised the TLS published it" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript).
  - **Pelling's rejection:** the narrative was "culled from those parts of commentators' reading lists that caught his eye". He asked how Gibbs avoided Feely's 1943 abbreviated-Latin errors, and called the approach "hogwash". He still thinks abbreviation is a reasonable idea in general — [Cipher Mysteries](https://ciphermysteries.com/?p=10022).
  - A further objection: the theory does not explain the labels — (search summary).

### Inferences
- Peer review has not filtered out bad claims. Both peer-reviewed claims (Hauer & Kondrak in computational linguistics, Cheshire in Romance philology) were in journals whose reviewers did not specialise in the manuscript. Experts rejected Cheshire within days, which suggests peer review outside the specialist field is weak evidence here.
- Latin, Hebrew and Turkic recur because each offers a ready way to absorb mismatches: abbreviation for Latin, unwritten vowels for Hebrew, agglutinative suffixes for Turkic.
- The number of claims is rising. The master list grew to 65+, and 2025–26 forum moderation now has to screen out AI-generated "solutions".

## What criteria do experts use to judge a proposed solution?

### Takeaway
Experts converge on a small set of tests. A solution must be reproducible by someone else using a fixed key. It must read continuous text, not just labels, into grammatical sentences that make sense for the period. It must fit the physical and codicological evidence, such as the radiocarbon date and the scribes. It must explain the text's anomalous statistics, especially its low character entropy. Claims with many free choices (anagrams, dropped vowels, several source languages, one-to-many glyph mappings) fail these tests by construction.

### Cited Findings
- **Lisa Fagin Davis, "What Will It Take to Solve the Voynich Manuscript?", *Manuscripts* (2020):**
  - "An acceptable proposal must result in a reading that makes sense semantically, chronologically, and logically" (p. 81).
  - "An acceptable proposal must also be consistent with the scientific evidence."
  - On the forum she added: "It must be in accordance with the evidence, it must be repeatable."
  - [Voynich Ninja tid=3257](https://www.voynich.ninja/printthread.php?tid=3257).
  - A secondary summary lists her requirements as sound first principles, reproducibility by others, conformance to linguistic and codicological facts, sense-making text, and logical text–illustration correspondence — [search summary](https://archive.org/details/voynich_MS_408/mode/2up).
- Davis also says the problem needs "a whole interdisciplinary team", and that computing advances let claims be critiqued "in a sophisticated and almost inarguable way" — [Undark](https://undark.org/2020/02/12/decoding-bizarre-voynich-manuscript/).
- **Zandbergen** judges claims by whether they address the text's measured features, chiefly the "anomalously low" bigram entropy, and whether results hold up across transcriptions — [Klausis Krypto Kolumne](https://scienceblogs.de/klausis-krypto-kolumne/2018/02/05/voynich-manuscript-errrm-no-not-really-sorry-but/). In his HistoCrypt 2019 summary he reportedly argues that the more useful question is how the text was produced. Any explanation must account for the regular word structure, since most words can be built from a small set of syllables — [Klausis Krypto Kolumne 2019-08-03](https://scienceblogs.de/klausis-krypto-kolumne/2019/08/03/rene-zandbergens-views-about-the-voynich-manuscript/) (search summary).
- **Claire Bowern** links to a criteria thread on Bluesky that I could not open. She sends theory authors to voynich.ninja, where they "will likely get a response, unless it's AI generated" — [Bowern's Yale page](https://campuspress.yale.edu/clairebowern/voynich).
- **Forum taxonomy (Mark Knowles, 2021):** symbol = word, symbol = letter (simple substitution is the most common), or hidden microscopic features. Knowles also asks whether a claim is complete or a partial crib, and counts its degrees of freedom: spelling variation, arbitrary spacing, little grammar, multiple languages, anagrams. Partial claims are harder to debunk. R. Sale asks which came first in the mapping, the glyphs or the language — [Voynich Ninja 3692](https://www.voynich.ninja/archive/index.php/thread-3692-2.html).
- **Pelling** asks whether a claim learns from earlier failures, for example Gibbs versus Feely, and whether it cites related work — [Cipher Mysteries](https://ciphermysteries.com/?p=10022); [Klausis](https://scienceblogs.de/klausis-krypto-kolumne/2018/02/05/voynich-manuscript-errrm-no-not-really-sorry-but/).
- **SantaColoma's practical test:** a blind trial in which two independent people apply the same key to the same passage — [Klausis 2020](https://scienceblogs.de/klausis-krypto-kolumne/2020/06/17/yet-another-voynich-manuscript-solution/).
- **Koen Gheuens** tests against the history of linguistics, flagging goropism and nationalist "Sun Language"-style reasoning (Ardıç) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript). After a 2023 Davis lecture he summarised: "when someone tells you they solved the Voynich, be very cautious" — [Voynich Ninja tid=4068](https://www.voynich.ninja/thread-4068.html).

### Inferences
- These criteria point to a single operational test: publish a fixed glyph-to-letter table, let a third party apply it blind to an unseen page, and see whether grammatical text in the claimed language comes out. None of the catalogued claims has publicly passed it.

## Which structural properties of Voynichese make natural-language readings hard, and how do proponents respond?

### Takeaway
Voynichese is more predictable at the character level than any known natural language. Its second-order conditional entropy (h2) is about 2 bits, against about 3–4 for natural languages. Its words follow a rigid slot-like template, some glyphs appear only in certain word or line positions, it has almost no one-letter words or words longer than ten letters, and it repeats identical or near-identical words in a row. Proponents usually answer with mechanisms that add degrees of freedom: abbreviation, dropped vowels, anagramming, phonetic or "poetic" orthography, or agglutinative suffixes. Critics note that each of these either makes the reading irreproducible or still fails to match the measured entropy.

### Cited Findings
- **Entropy:** Voynichese h2 ≈ 2, versus 3–4 for natural languages, while higher-level properties look language-like. Bowern concludes it is more likely an encoded or constructed natural language than a substitution or polyalphabetic cipher — [Wikipedia summarising Lindemann & Bowern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript).
- Lindemann & Bowern's abstract says the low predictability is not explained by script, transcription system, underlying language or substitution cipher, and that "substantial manipulations of glyph composition are not sufficient to align conditional entropy levels with natural languages". They suggest tight within-word placement constraints may reflect lost phonemic distinctions — [arXiv 2010.14697](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14697).
- Word entropy is about 10 bits per word, similar to English or Latin. Zipf-like rank frequencies hold — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript).
- **Word structure:** some glyphs occur only word-initially, medially or finally, in prefix–root–suffix-like slots. Rubio: grammatical-marker-like letters "never appear in the middle of 'words'", which is "unheard of for any Indo-European, Hungarian, or Finnish language" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript).
- **Missing short words:** "practically no words have fewer than two letters or more than ten" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript). This means there are almost none of the short function words (articles, prepositions, conjunctions) typical of European languages.
- **Repetition:** the same common word can appear "up to five times in a row", and words differing by one letter sit side by side unusually often — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript).
- **Line effects:** Currier (1976) called the line "a functional entity". Frequencies at line starts and ends differ markedly from line-internal ones; some characters never begin a line, and others begin a line's first word at about 1/100 of the expected rate (samples up to 15,000 words). [m] and [g] prefer line ends — [search summary of Stolfi notes / CEUR-WS Vol-3313](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-3313/paper12.pdf). Counterpoint: some forum analysts argue that ordinary line-breaking bias produces part of this effect in any language with variable word lengths — [search summary, Voynich Ninja](https://www.voynich.ninja/archive/index.php/thread-5718-5.html). Whether lines are truly functional units is still disputed.
- **Currier A/B:** two statistically distinct "languages" (hands or dialects) with substantially different common vocabularies, split by manuscript section — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript).
- Older verdicts quoted by Timm: D'Imperio, "the text just doesn't act like natural language"; Tiltman, "Languages simply do not behave in this way" — [Voynich Ninja tid=3257](https://www.voynich.ninja/printthread.php?tid=3257).
- **Proponent responses:**
  - **Anagrams/alphagrams (Hauer & Kondrak).** Zandbergen: earlier anagram experiments did not reproduce the low entropy — [Klausis](https://scienceblogs.de/klausis-krypto-kolumne/2018/02/05/voynich-manuscript-errrm-no-not-really-sorry-but/).
  - **Abbreviation (Gibbs, Feely; Pelling allows abbreviation might produce the binomial word-length distribution)** — [Cipher Mysteries](https://ciphermysteries.com/?p=10022).
  - **Unvoweled Semitic (Hannig, Stojko for Ukrainian).** Lasry: this gives too much interpretive freedom — [Klausis 2020](https://scienceblogs.de/klausis-krypto-kolumne/2020/06/17/yet-another-voynich-manuscript-solution/).
  - **Agglutinative suffixes and repetition as "poetic" style (Ardıç).** A forum critic answered that repetition is not distinctively Turkic, citing refrains in Slovenian folk songs — [search summary, Voynich Ninja 2318](https://www.voynich.ninja/archive/index.php/thread-2318-43.html).
  - **Multi-phthongs and no double consonants (Cheshire).** Petersen: the letter distribution is still unlike Romance — [ZME Science](https://zmescience.com/science/british-scolar-voynich-manuscript-decoded-0423432).
  - **Amancio et al. (2013)** found the word statistics compatible with natural language in about 90% of measures. This is often cited by language-claim proponents, but it is not a decipherment — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript); [Undark](https://undark.org/2020/02/12/decoding-bizarre-voynich-manuscript/).

### Inferences
- If a plaintext language had h2 near 2, any letter-for-letter substitution would keep that value. Simple-substitution claims, the most common kind, therefore imply a natural language with unprecedentedly low h2, and none of the proposed languages has one. Each claim must either add a lossy or verbose layer, which undercuts the "it's simply language X" framing, or leave the statistics unexplained. Most do the latter.

- I did not get Davis's 2020 five-scribe paleography details from a primary source in this session.

## Has any language claim made a falsifiable prediction that was tested?

### Takeaway
Very few have, and none survived. The nearest things to tests were third parties applying a published key: Hansen applying the Tucker/Janick Nahuatl key, and Petersen and others checking Cheshire's mappings. Both produced inconsistent or non-language output. Hauer & Kondrak's Hebrew identification is in principle a testable prediction, but it was never confirmed by Hebrew specialists, and Zandbergen showed the model leaves out the entropy evidence. Most claims are framed as partial or open-ended, so they cannot be refuted.

### Cited Findings
- Hansen applied the 2018 Nahuatl key and got repetitive, glossolalia-like output, and more than half the plant names start with /a/ — [search summary of Cipher Mysteries](https://ciphermysteries.com/?p=11719).
- Petersen found Cheshire's scheme reads the same word differently in different places and yields "almost no grammar" — [ZME Science](https://zmescience.com/science/british-scolar-voynich-manuscript-decoded-0423432). A reproducer said that after applying Cheshire's substitutions "you have no choice but to be subjective" — [search summary](https://www.sciencealert.com/?p=41286).
- Hauer & Kondrak's language ID is a concrete, checkable output: Hebrew ranked first of 380 — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/Q16-1006/). The authors did not consult Hebrew scholars, and critics said the words do not mean what was claimed — [search summary of Mosaic / Atlas Obscura](https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/voynich-manuscript-artificial-intelligence-solved). Its results also vary with the transcription — [Klausis](https://scienceblogs.de/klausis-krypto-kolumne/2018/02/05/voynich-manuscript-errrm-no-not-really-sorry-but/).
- Tucker & Talbert's New World dating (1521–c.1576) amounts to a prediction about the manuscript's age. It is contradicted by radiocarbon dating of the vellum to the early 15th century. The radiocarbon dates are covered in the physical-evidence notes; I did not re-source them here.
- SantaColoma proposed a blind two-reader test for Hannig's Hebrew. I found no record that it was run — [Klausis 2020](https://scienceblogs.de/klausis-krypto-kolumne/2020/06/17/yet-another-voynich-manuscript-solution/).
- Bax's method predicts that further plant and star labels should decode consistently with his 14 glyph values. He died before extending it, and I found no documented systematic test by others — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript).
- Ardıç's team claims to read 30%+ of the manuscript, but critics say it has not released the full 60+ combination-glyph table, which would be needed for anyone to test it — [Cipher Mysteries](https://ciphermysteries.com/?p=10783).

### Inferences
- **Recurring reasons claims fail, synthesised from the cited critiques:**
  1. **Too many degrees of freedom.** Anagrams, dropped vowels, multiple languages, one-to-many glyph values and abbreviations each make the output underdetermined. A reader can then get "sense" out of any text (Hauer & Kondrak, Hannig, Cheshire, Levitov, Stojko).
  2. **Circular cribbing.** A plant or label is identified from the picture, the glyph values are set from that guess, and the drawing is then cited as confirmation (Cheshire, Bax, Tucker & Talbert). Davis's "circular and aspirational".
  3. **No grammatical running text.** Output is lists of words or label-like glosses, not sentences (Gibbs, Cheshire, Hannig).
  4. **Not reproducible.** No full key is published, or the key needs subjective choices (Ardıç, Czech teams, Cheshire).
  5. **The statistics are ignored.** Low h2 entropy, the slot grammar, the missing short words, line and position effects, and the Currier A/B split go unexplained (Zandbergen on Hauer & Kondrak; Lindemann & Bowern).
  6. **Linguistic or historical impossibility.** An undocumented "proto-Romance", a New World origin against the radiocarbon date, nationalist etymologies (Cheshire, Tucker, Ardıç).
  7. **Wrong reviewers or wrong venue.** Peer review outside Voynich studies (Cheshire, Hauer & Kondrak), or no review at all (TLS, YouTube, Zenodo, festivals).
  8. **No engagement with prior failures** (Pelling on Gibbs versus Feely; on Hauer & Kondrak's missing related work).

### Gaps
- I found no documented case of a natural-language claim publishing a prediction in advance (for example, "page X will read Y") and then having it tested blind.
