# What the world knows about the Voynich Manuscript (public-evidence baseline)

Author: Sylvie (Jon's assistant), relayed by Jon 2026-10-17 22:07 PDT. Saved verbatim by voynich-08. Scope as Sylvie set
it: public, verifiable sources only; excludes this project's unpublished results and proposed explanations. The source
links Sylvie cited were not opened by this session (unverified here); they are named in brackets where she named them.

1. It is a physical, illustrated parchment book held at Yale. Catalogue name Beinecke MS 408. Yale records 102 parchment
   leaves plus a paper leaf, with several foldouts and missing leaves. A leaf has two sides; foldout panels and digital
   images create different page counts. [Yale catalogue]
2. Its parchment dates to the early 1400s. University of Arizona radiocarbon testing placed the animal-skin material in
   the early fifteenth century. This dates the parchment, not the exact moment the text was written. [University of
   Arizona research announcement]
3. Its materials have been examined scientifically. Laboratory testing identified iron-gall ink in sampled writing and
   drawings, and azurite in sampled blue pigment. These are findings about the tested material; they do not reveal the
   text's meaning or provide an exact writing date. [Laboratory report hosted by Yale]
4. The author and the meaning of the main text remain unknown. Yale describes the script as unidentified and the text as
   undeciphered. The book's modern name comes from Wilfrid Voynich, who acquired it in 1912, not from a known author.
   H. P. Kraus donated it to Yale in 1969. [Beinecke overview]
5. The pictures are visible; their relationship to the text is unresolved. They include plants, star and zodiac diagrams,
   human figures in pools or connected forms, jars, roots, and a large circular foldout. Names such as "herbal",
   "bathing" and "pharmaceutical" describe groups of illustrations. They are not translations of the accompanying text.
   [Beinecke overview]
6. Its history is partly documented. Evidence includes an ownership inscription associated with Jacobus de Tepenec and a
   surviving letter from Johannes Marcus Marci to Athanasius Kircher. The familiar story that Emperor Rudolf II paid 600
   ducats appears in that correspondence. It should be identified as a historical report, rather than a surviving
   purchase record. [Catalogue and letter transcription]
7. The writing has measurable patterns. The symbols do not occur equally often or combine freely. Some combinations are
   common; others are rare or absent. Researchers have described recurring structures within the space-separated symbol
   groups. Calling those groups "words" is convenient, but does not establish that they are words in a known language.
   [Published slot-structure analysis]
8. Symbol use changes with position on the page. Certain symbols and combinations occur disproportionately at line
   beginnings, line endings, and particular paragraph positions. Published research also documents differences within
   line interiors. The existence of these patterns is distinct from knowing why they occur. [Feaster's positional analysis]
9. Different parts of the manuscript have different text statistics. Researchers distinguish major textual varieties
   conventionally called Currier A and Currier B. These labels describe observed differences; they do not identify two
   decoded languages. Analyses also depend on which transcription and symbol-grouping conventions are used.
   [Comparative text study]
10. Its symbol sequences are unusually predictable in published comparisons. Studies find low conditional character
    entropy: knowing preceding symbols makes the next symbol relatively predictable compared with the natural-language
    samples tested. This observation does not, by itself, establish a cipher, meaningful language, or meaningless text.
    [Entropy study]
11. Word-length dependence was documented before our project. Published studies have found relationships between the
    lengths of nearby space-separated groups. A 2022 study also measured word-length autocorrelation in human-produced
    meaningless writing. The result depends on the measurement, transcription and comparison used; it is not a
    translation. [Gaskell and Bowern]
12. Statistical structure alone does not establish meaning. Experiments show that people can produce meaningless text
    with several statistical properties also found in natural language or Voynich transcriptions. Conversely, those
    similarities do not establish that the Voynich Manuscript is meaningless. [Human-generated text study]
13. Transcriptions are research representations of the manuscript. Systems such as EVA replace manuscript glyphs with
    characters that computers can process. Those characters are labels, not established pronunciations. Different
    decisions about glyphs and boundaries affect some measurements. [Transcription and entropy analysis]
14. The original pages can be inspected online. Yale provides high-resolution scans. Access to the fragile physical
    manuscript is restricted. [Yale's access information and digital-manuscript link]

The unresolved questions. There is no established answer to who composed the main text, what its symbols represent,
what the book was intended to do, or how to read it. "A particular language", "a cipher", "music", "a medical manual",
and "a hoax" are proposed explanations, not facts to include as conclusions. [Yale's current description]

In one sentence: we know a considerable amount about the manuscript's physical form, parts of its history, and patterns
in its writing; its meaning remains unresolved.
