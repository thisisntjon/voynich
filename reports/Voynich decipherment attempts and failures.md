# Voynichese still defeats every reader, ourselves included

*Synthesised 2026-10-17 15:16 PDT (time read from the system clock), from the six research-note files in `research_notes/Voynich decipherment attempts and failures/`, our experiment write-up `docs/rounds/README-a-series.md` (a1–a7) and the B2–B5 results in `user_document_analysis.md`.*

As of October 2026 nobody has deciphered the Voynich manuscript, and nobody is measurably close. No reader has produced one sentence that a third party can regenerate from a fixed key. The **Voynich Ninja master list alone counts 65 language-claim threads**, and every one is rejected ([Voynich Ninja](https://www.voynich.ninja/printthread.php?tid=4339)). What has advanced in the past century is the set of constraints a solution must satisfy, not the reading. The vellum dates to **1404–1438** ([voynich.nu](https://www.voynich.nu/extra/carbon.html)). Its character-level predictability is lower than that of any of 294 tested languages ([Lindemann & Bowern](https://lingbuzz.net/lingbuzz/005522/v1.pdf)). It was written by about five scribes, and its page order is not the original one ([Layfield & Davis 2026](https://journals.openedition.org/digitalmedievalist/2331)). Attempts fail for the same reasons again and again. There is no crib. Claimed keys have enough free choices to make any text say anything. Simple substitution cannot change the anomalous entropy. And statistical "decipherments" are rarely run against meaningless controls. Three hypotheses remain live: a natural language written in a script that drops distinctions, a multi-step verbose cipher, and structured meaningless generation. As of 2026 none of them reproduces the full statistical profile. Our own controlled experiments confirm this picture and sharpen three constraints, but they read nothing. The user's document, "Analyzing the Voynich Manuscript.docx", is a secondary synthesis. Its claim to have "decoded" the text "at the structural level" is untested, and its main source contradicts it on key points.

## Sixty-five language claims, and none has passed a blind key test

The natural-language claims share one structure. Someone picks a target language, then adds enough flexibility to make Voynichese fit it. Newbold (1921) read Latin in microscopic "shorthand" strokes that **Manly showed in 1931 were ink cracking on the vellum**, and he unscrambled them with anagrams so free that almost any reading was possible ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript); [voynich.nu](https://www.voynich.nu/solvers.html)). Feely's abbreviated Latin (1943) was "unanimously rejected." Strong's 1947 cipher reading turned out, from his posthumous notes, to depend on a "questionably subjective" final stage ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript)). The modern claims repeat the pattern with better publicity.

Only two claims passed formal peer review, and both show that peer review outside Voynich studies is weak evidence. Hauer and Kondrak's language-identification method was **97% accurate across 380 languages** and pointed to Hebrew written as alphagrams ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/Q16-1006/)). Their sample sentence, though, came from a "couple of spelling corrections" followed by a pass through Google Translate ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript)). René Zandbergen answered that the paper ignores the most important feature of the text, its "anomalously low" bigram entropy, and that the results change with the transcription used ([Klausis Krypto Kolumne](https://scienceblogs.de/klausis-krypto-kolumne/2018/02/05/voynich-manuscript-errrm-no-not-really-sorry-but/)). Gerard Cheshire's "calligraphic proto-Romance" (*Romance Studies*, 2019) collapsed within days. Lisa Fagin Davis called it "circular and aspirational", adding that "proto-Romance language is not a thing" ([ZME Science](https://zmescience.com/science/british-scolar-voynich-manuscript-decoded-0423432)). **Bristol withdrew its press release** and disowned the work ([Higgypop](https://higgypop.com/news/bristol-uni-voynich-manuscript)). Davis's diagnosis was that the reviewers were Romance specialists, not Voynich specialists ([Undark](https://undark.org/2020/02/12/decoding-bizarre-voynich-manuscript/)).

Most of the remaining well-known claims were never peer-reviewed. Stephen Bax (2014) read **10 words from 14 glyph values** by matching plant and star names to the pictures. Forums treat this as a "partial crib", not a decipherment, and it was never extended before his death in 2017 ([Bax PDF](https://stephenbax.net/wp-content/uploads/2014/01/Voynich-a-provisional-partial-decoding-BAX.pdf); [Voynich Ninja](https://www.voynich.ninja/archive/index.php/thread-3692-2.html)). Tucker and Talbert proposed Nahuatl. Nahuatl specialist Magnus Pharao Hansen called it "pure speculation", and their implied date of 1521 or later is contradicted by the radiocarbon result ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript)). Nicholas Gibbs's abbreviated-Latin women's-health reading (TLS, 2017) "doesn't result in Latin that makes sense," according to Davis ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript)). The Ardıç family's Old Turkic claim says it covers "over 30%" of the text. It has continued into a February 2026 paper from a Turkish board, but the team never released its table of **60+ "combination" glyphs**, so no one can test it ([Cipher Mysteries](https://ciphermysteries.com/?p=10783); [Voynich Ninja](https://www.voynich.ninja/printthread.php?tid=5401)). Rainer Hannig's unvoweled Hebrew (2020) drew George Lasry's objection that dropped vowels give too much freedom. Richard SantaColoma proposed a blind test with two independent readers. As far as we found, it was never run ([Klausis Krypto Kolumne](https://scienceblogs.de/klausis-krypto-kolumne/2020/06/17/yet-another-voynich-manuscript-solution/)). Claims from 2024 to 2026 include Matarrese's Friulian-German festival talk, a Zenodo "99.8% confidence" package, and a stream of AI-generated forum posts. They add volume without adding rigour. The master list now explicitly excludes AI-assisted solutions ([Voynich Ninja](https://www.voynich.ninja/printthread.php?tid=4339)).

The experts' criteria explain why all of these fail. Davis requires a reading that "makes sense semantically, chronologically, and logically" and is "consistent with the scientific evidence" and repeatable ([Voynich Ninja](https://www.voynich.ninja/printthread.php?tid=3257)). Zandbergen requires that a claim address the measured statistics. Together these amount to one operational test: publish a fixed glyph table, let someone else apply it blind to a page they have not seen, and check whether grammatical text comes out. No claim has passed it. The deeper problem is mathematical. A consistent letter-for-letter substitution leaves conditional entropy unchanged. A substitution claim therefore implies a plaintext language whose h2 is near 2 bits. The lowest of 294 languages measured is 2.85 ([Lindemann & Bowern](https://lingbuzz.net/lingbuzz/005522/v1.pdf)). Latin's abbreviations, Hebrew's missing vowels and Turkic's suffixes recur in these claims because each gives the claimant room to absorb mismatches. That same room makes the claim impossible to falsify.

## Professional codebreakers narrowed the field to "an intermediate step"

The NSA-lineage analysts reached a negative result, but a narrow one. William Friedman's study groups (1944–46 and 1962–63) transliterated the text onto punch cards ([voynich.nu](https://www.voynich.nu/solvers.html)). Friedman's private conclusion, hidden as an anagram in a footnote, was that the manuscript was "an early attempt to construct an artificial or universal language of the a priori type" ([Tablet](https://www.tabletmag.com/sections/arts-letters/articles/cracking-the-voynich-code)). John Tiltman mapped the rigid structure of beginnings, middles and endings in the words. He concluded the text "cannot be the result of substituting single symbols for letters in the natural order," and described "a cumbersome mixture of different types of substitution" ([Cipher Mysteries](https://ciphermysteries.com/tag/john-tiltman)). Prescott Currier found two statistical "languages", A and B, tied to different hands. He also found that the line behaves as a functional unit: **in about 25,000 words, no repeated sequence crosses a line break**. His verdict was that "there has to be an intermediate step somewhere" and that the units are "_not words!_" ([voynich.nu](https://www.voynich.nu/extra/curr_main.html)). Mary D'Imperio's 1978 monograph collected all of this and remained the standard reference for about 30 years, without a solution.

Entropy is what removes the classic cipher families. Bennett's figures put Voynich h2 at **2.22 bits, against 3.01–3.37 for European languages** ([voynich.nu](https://voynich.nu/a2_char.html)). Lindemann and Bowern found Voynich lower than every one of 316 comparison texts. They trace the cause to common glyphs being restricted to fixed positions within the word ([lingbuzz](https://lingbuzz.net/lingbuzz/005522/v1.pdf)). Simple substitution fails by construction. Periodic polyalphabetic ciphers flatten frequencies and raise entropy, which is the opposite of what Voynich shows. Nomenclators are disfavoured by the small glyph inventory, though not strictly excluded ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript)). The family that survives is the verbose cipher, in which several glyphs stand for one plaintext letter. Nick Pelling has long argued that groups such as *qo*, *ol*, *ar* and *aiin* act this way ([Cipher Mysteries](https://ciphermysteries.com/?p=2056)).

The most important cipher result of 2024–26 is an existence proof, not a decipherment. Michael Greshko's **Naibbe cipher** (*Cryptologia*, 2025) is a verbose homophonic substitution. Dice split the plaintext into single letters and pairs, and playing cards choose one of six tables. It turns Latin or Italian into text with Voynich-like glyph frequencies, word lengths and slot behaviour, uses only early-15th-century materials, and can still be deciphered ([Sci.News](https://www.sci.news/othersciences/linguistics/voynich-manuscript-cipher-14466.html); [Schneier](https://www.schneier.com/blog/archives/2025/12/substitution-cipher-based-on-the-voynich-manuscript.html)). Its limits are exactly the old professional observations. It replicates Currier B only incompletely. It does not produce long-range correlations or line effects. It generates only about **45% of Voynich word types**, and its tables were filled with Voynich words. Torsten Timm calls that circular ([Voynich Ninja](https://www.voynich.ninja/archive/index.php/thread-5081-2.html)). Greshko himself says it "fails in several major ways" and does not claim the manuscript used it. The sources disagree on the online publication date: one note gives 26 November 2025, another about 26 September 2025.

Professionals gave their own reasons for failing. They had no crib and no parallel text. The glyph inventory and the word boundaries were uncertain, so every transliteration builds in assumptions. The statistics defeat standard frequency methods. Elizebeth Friedman called statistical analysis "doomed to utter frustration" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript)). The crib problem is decisive for the cipher hypothesis that remains. A verbose homophonic system with randomised table choice has a key space far larger than the roughly 38,000 tokens available. Without a known plaintext word, any "solution" is underdetermined, the same failure that sank Newbold and Strong.

## Meaningless-text models match the low-level statistics but miss the large-scale structure

The hoax side has grown more sophisticated, but it has not closed its case. Gordon Rugg showed that a table of syllables read through a Cardan grille quickly produces Voynich-like text. Rugg and Taylor (2016) argued that the main statistical oddities could come from such a low-tech hoax ([Cipher Mysteries](https://ciphermysteries.com/2016/09/14/gordon-rugg-man-cracked-mystery-voynich-manuscript-cracks-not)). Two problems remain. The grille dates to around 1550, a century after the vellum. And the fit depends on hand-tuned tables plus ad hoc rules for line starts and ends. A 2026 preprint by Parisel tests a Rugg-style grille and a slot generator against four signatures jointly and finds that **"neither class reproduces all four signatures simultaneously"** ([arXiv 2604.19762](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19762)). Timm and Schinner's self-citation algorithm copies earlier words with small mutations (*Cryptologia*, 2020). It is the most developed meaningless generator and reproduces Zipf's laws and the similarity of neighbouring words. A 2021 forum test, though, found its line-position plots flat, while real Voynich pages show strong positional preferences ([Voynich Ninja](https://www.voynich.ninja/archive/index.php/thread-3647.html)).

Gaskell and Bowern (2022) provided the strongest empirical support for meaninglessness. They had **42 volunteers handwrite gibberish**. The result resembled Voynichese on 41 of 42 metrics: low information content, repetition, and positive autocorrelation of word lengths. A random-forest classifier placed all five Voynich transcriptions nearer to gibberish than to 75 meaningful texts, "albeit with low confidence" ([CEUR-WS](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-3313/paper4.pdf)). The authors concede that their short samples cannot test section-level vocabulary, which "pose[s] a serious challenge" to the hoax hypothesis. On the other side, Montemurro and Zanette found that section-specific keywords carry information at a scale of **600–800 words**, the same scale as natural languages ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3689824)). Sterneck, Polish and Bowern found topic clusters that align with "a conjunction of scribe and subject matter" ([Yale PDF](https://alumniacademy.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2021-07/Topic%20Modeling%20in%20the%20Voynich%20Manuscript.pdf)). Both results share a confound. Scribes line up with sections, so "keywords cluster by section" could reflect five writers' habits rather than five topics.

| Property | Rugg grille | Timm & Schinner | Human gibberish | Naibbe cipher |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low h2, Zipf, slot-like words | claimed | claimed | yes | many reproduced |
| Adjacent-word similarity | partial | yes (core mechanism) | yes | not reported |
| Currier A/B | via switching tables | not verified | untested | B incomplete |
| Line-start and line-end effects | ad hoc rules only | **no** | not distinctive | out of scope |
| Section keywords | untested | not verified | too short to test | not reported |
| Joint positional test (Parisel 2026) | **fails** | untested | untested | untested |

The low-level statistics no longer discriminate between the hypotheses. Gibberish, self-citation and a verbose cipher can all produce low entropy, Zipf behaviour and slot-like words. The discriminating evidence has moved to large-scale and positional structure, and no model passes all of it. The meaningless side must explain section vocabulary and line effects with a procedure plausible for the period. The meaningful side must explain h2 near 2 bits, and it still has no reading.

## Physical evidence fixes when and how the book was made, but not what it says

The material evidence has removed whole classes of theory without supporting any reading. Four radiocarbon samples were taken from Herbal-A, Herbal-B and an astronomical foldout. They agree closely, at **1404–1438 at 95% confidence**, which points to one stock of parchment for the whole book ([voynich.nu](https://www.voynich.nu/extra/carbon.html)). McCrone's analysis found iron-gall ink and period pigments with "no modern components" ([AIC](https://resources.culturalheritage.org/conservators-converse/tag/carbon-14-dating/)). Zandbergen stresses that neither test dates the writing itself. The 2009 claim that the ink was applied immediately after the parchment was made "did not" follow from the analysis. This rules out Roger Bacon, a forgery by Voynich, and very probably a Kelley-era fabrication. It also makes the New World readings chronologically very hard. A 15th-century hoax or meaningless text remains possible.

Davis's palaeography identified **five scribes**, and nearly every bifolio is the work of one of them. Scribe 1 writes Language A and the others write B ([Layfield & Davis 2026](https://journals.openedition.org/digitalmedievalist/2331)). Currier had estimated six to eight, and Timm argued in 2026 that scribes, like the languages, sit on a continuum rather than in discrete categories. Davis now says "the 'lone genius' idea is kind of out of the door" ([IFLScience](https://www.iflscience.com/we-may-have-been-reading-the-voynich-manuscript-in-the-wrong-order-for-over-a-century-84870)). The most recent codicological result is Layfield and Davis's "Singulion Structure" (*Digital Medievalist*, July 2026). They argue the book was made as loose single bifolia read in sequence, and that its only 15th-century sewing was itself a misbinding ([Digital Medievalist](https://journals.openedition.org/digitalmedievalist/2331)). Timm accepts that texts are more similar within a physical bifolio, but calls the proposed reading order "underdetermined by the data" ([Voynich Ninja](https://www.voynich.ninja/archive/index.php/thread-5911.html)). In practice, the text available to any solver is out of order and incomplete. About 102 of perhaps 116 folios survive, and two zodiac signs are missing ([voynich.nu](https://www.voynich.nu/descr.html)).

The extra-textual layers looked like possible cribs, and they have not delivered. The 2024 multispectral release covers ten leaves. It found **no palimpsest and no hidden key** ([Davis 2024](https://manuscriptroadtrip.wordpress.com/2024/09/08/multispectral-imaging-and-the-voynich-manuscript/)). It revealed alphabet columns on f1r, which Davis attributes to Marci (1662–65) as an early decoding attempt "unlikely to help" ([The Art Newspaper](https://theartnewspaper.com/2024/09/25/voynich-manuscript-scans-reveal-early-decoding-attempt)). It also changed letter readings in the f116v marginalia, so keys built on the old transcriptions are unreliable. The zodiac month names are Romance and were probably added by a later 15th-century reader, not the author ([voynich.nu](https://www.voynich.nu/history.html)). On provenance, Stefan Guzy's 2023 discovery of a 1599 sale of rare books for 600 florins, from Carl Widemann to Rudolf II, strengthens the Rudolf link without proving it ([CEUR-WS](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-3313/paper16.pdf)). The total documentary gap between about 1430 and 1599 remains.

## Computation since 2019 has changed the question from "which language?" to "which process?"

Recent quantitative work is more rigorous and more modest. Lindemann and Bowern found that transcription choice has a "negligible" effect on entropy, while the way glyphs are grouped shifts h2 by about 0.27 bits. No grouping closes the gap: Voynich h2 ranges from **1.95 to 2.50** across subsets, against a floor of 2.85 for natural languages. In Voynich, **11 bigrams with conditional probability above 50% make up 29.5% of the text, against 3.1% for English** ([lingbuzz](https://lingbuzz.net/lingbuzz/005522/v1.pdf)). Their preferred explanation is a script that ignores some sound distinctions of the underlying language. The closest natural texts are tonal languages written without tone marks. Zattera's slot model fits **86.6% of tokens to 12 slots** ([CEUR-WS](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-3313/paper10.pdf)). Feaster's method showed that a word's glyph makeup depends on its position in the line ([CEUR-WS](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-3313/paper12.pdf)).

The 2026 arXiv wave goes further and questions whether the units are words at all. Rozanova and Temerev, using matched controls that include Naibbe ciphertext, find that **one token predicts the next by less than 1% of token entropy** (controls 2–10%). They also find that about 70% of token types occur only once, and that glyphs on either side of a space share about 0.2 bits of mutual information. Their summary: "a token is not a word, a space is not a space" ([arXiv 2608.17096](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17096)). Their h2 figure, about 2.7 against about 3.5 for Latin, is higher than Lindemann and Bowern's 2.16. The difference probably comes from how glyphs are grouped and whether spaces count; the gap to natural languages persists either way. Naibbe and self-citation output reproduce the low entropy and the weak word order, but not this edge coupling. That makes edge coupling a useful discriminating benchmark. Matthew Schwartz's October 2026 preprint is self-described as preliminary and partly AI-generated. It reports that readings in 18 languages fail held-out tests, and that a keyless reuse-and-coinage procedure reproduces most summary statistics. It leaves only "two weak traces of content": label words, and a subject effect of about **0.2–0.3 bits per page** ([Schwartz 2026](https://bootloops.ai/voynich.pdf)).

Machine learning and LLM claims fail one evaluation in particular: they are not run against negative controls. Language identification always returns some language. Shlomo Argamon noted that Google Translate produces coherent output even from meaningless input, which leaves "huge latitude" ([Mental Floss](https://www.mentalfloss.com/science/archaeology/researchers-claim-crack-voynich-manuscript-using-ai-experts-are-skeptical)). Hauer and Kondrak's method was never tested on known-gibberish text ([Voynich Ninja](https://www.voynich.ninja/printthread.php?tid=2262)). Self-published LLM-era decipherments on Zenodo and PyPI are unreviewed. Forum members describe one as "hallucinating hundreds of obviously wrong claims" ([Voynich Ninja](https://www.voynich.ninja/printthread.php?tid=5382)). Since Gaskell and Bowern showed that gibberish matches Voynichese on most metrics, a statistical claim without a gibberish control has no evidential value.

## Our experiments reproduce the consensus and sharpen three constraints, but neither they nor the user's document read anything

Our a1–a7 runs differ from most amateur work in one way: every test runs against natural-language controls, and the solver had to crack a known cipher before its Voynich output was read. The headline numbers match the literature. **Voynich h2 is 2.13 against 3.11–3.30 for the controls**, and collapsing digraphs only lifts it to 2.26–2.40 ([README](../docs/rounds/README-a-series.md)). This agrees with Lindemann and Bowern's 2.16 and their small effect of glyph grouping. For line position (a2), the first-glyph JSD is **0.176 against a shuffle null of 0.002**, with line-initial *p* over-represented 17.9× and line-final *m* 16×. That puts a number on Currier's "functional line" ([results_a2.txt](../results_a2.txt)). The decisive negative is a6. A simulated-annealing homophonic solver recovered **100% of a Caesar text enciphered with a random 21-symbol key**. On Voynich it landed **0.7–1.7 bits per character below every same-language ceiling**, and from k = 8 upward it was within about 0.1 bit of a meaningless pseudo-Voynich null ([README](../docs/rounds/README-a-series.md)). Within its scope, this rules out letter-for-unit substitution of Latin, Italian or German with Voynich word breaks kept. That agrees with Schwartz's held-out failures by a different solver family. The a5 test shows that merging glyph pairs until h2 reaches the Latin level leaves **2.5–2.6 units per word**. If Voynich words are plaintext words, those units behave like syllables or code fragments, not letters, which is consistent with Rozanova and Temerev's "a token is not a word." The other tests were weaker. Labels showed no significant link to their pages (a3, p ≈ 0.11 on only 40 pages). Self-citation locality was modest at 1.30 after matching on language, section and hand (a4). The law of abbreviation was weak (a7).

The B2–B5 tests, built to check the user's document, add three results not found in the notes' literature, at least in this form. First, the slot rule *qo-* → gallows is a genuine second-order dependency. P(gallows | qo-) is 0.841 against 0.403 under a bigram null, so it is not a bigram artefact ([results_b3.txt](../results_b3.txt)). Second, section vocabulary survives within a single scribe. Hand 2 gives NMI 0.333 with a null 99th percentile of 0.104, though against a stricter contiguous-rotation null the margin narrows to p = 0.048. A page-local self-citation copier passed through the same pipeline does not reproduce it ([results_b5.txt](../results_b5.txt)). Third, line-edge effects never emerge from copying alone. A Timm-style generator gives JSD 0.0006–0.0010, equal to its null, and *p* ×1.0–1.2. Seeding each page with its real first line raises *p* only to ×1.5–1.8, against ×16.0 in the real text, even though the simulator is more copy-heavy than Voynich ([results_b4.txt](../results_b4.txt)). These results cut against the README's own tentative lean towards generated text. A meaningless generator now needs explicit line rules and section-specific seed vocabulary. The verbose-cipher family likewise needs positional machinery that Naibbe lacks. Each family survives only by adding the structure it was meant to explain.

The user's document is a confident, apparently AI-assisted digest. Its main quantitative source is Schwartz's provisional preprint, and it goes beyond that source on two key points. It says slot-1 prefixes encode section-specific "operational classes" and that morphology "maps deterministically" to subject matter. Schwartz reports that "tests for section-specific grammar ... are negative," and that the herbal section shows no subject tint at all ([Schwartz 2026](https://bootloops.ai/voynich.pdf)). Two of the document's supporting arguments fail on our data. Its claim that a word shuffle "proves" a word-internal origin is a tautology: with the space counted as a symbol, a word shuffle changes h2 by exactly 0.000 in any text, Voynich or control. Its two-state HMM split into word-final and other glyphs did not reproduce. Baum-Welch instead finds a vowel-like class {o, a, e, y}, and the partition is unstable across restarts ([results_b2.txt](../results_b2.txt)). It describes 74% Sukhotin alternation as "low", but the controls sit at 0.70–0.82. It says ink was "proven" contemporaneous and that the merlons "pinpoint" the origin, both of which the sources dispute. It also misattributes Gaskell and Bowern's gibberish study as verbose-cipher work. What holds in it, including the date, the milieu, the positional rules, the A/B signatures and the rejection of Bax and Cheshire, was already published. Neither the document nor our experiments gets closer to a reading than the state of the art. Ours adds controlled, reproducible negatives and a sharper benchmark. The document adds an interpretation that no one has tested.

## Conclusion

The Voynich problem has turned from a decoding puzzle into a model-selection problem. The usable output of a century of failure is a benchmark: low h2, slot words, Currier A/B, ×16 line-edge enrichment, edge coupling across spaces, and section vocabulary that survives within a single scribe. Every serious hypothesis now clears part of it by construction and fails the rest. That changes what progress looks like. Another reading in another language counts for nothing until a fixed key is published and tested blind. A generator or cipher that reproduces the whole benchmark from a 15th-century-plausible procedure would be real news, even without plaintext, because it would show which kind of object the manuscript is. In that sense the most consequential recent work, Naibbe, Rozanova and Temerev, Parisel, and Layfield and Davis, is about constraints rather than readings.

For our own line of work, the cheapest next tests are the ones that separate the three live families, not new language attempts. A quire-aligned null for B5 would test whether the within-scribe section signal is writing-order drift. A generator whose seed vocabulary changes by section would show whether it can reach the observed 0.27–0.33 within-hand NMI. B4-style generators should be scored against Rozanova and Temerev's edge-coupling target, and Naibbe ciphertext should be run through our a2 and B4 line tests. If a section-seeded generator with simple line rules matches all of these, the meaningless hypothesis becomes the most economical explanation. If it cannot, the case for an encoded text, perhaps syllabic or verbose, gets stronger, still without a key. One caveat matters more than any single number. The corpus is incomplete, its page order is not the author's, and its word boundaries are uncertain. Every statistic here, ours included, rests on a transcription that builds in judgments about what counts as a glyph and where a word ends.
